\chapter{Introduction\label{chap:intro}}

\section{Ultra-High Energy Neutrinos and Cosmic Rays}

  Neutrinos are neutral leptons, nearly massless, rarely interact, and exist in 3 flavors akin to their charged lepton counterparts: the electron, muon, and tau.
  Neutrinos are one of the four messengers used to study multi-messenger astrophysics: photons, cosmic rays, gravitational waves, and neutrinos.
  Photons (i.e. radio waves, optical light, X-rays, and gamma-rays) have been used in astronomy and astrophysics for over 10,000 years~\citep{snowmass_gammaray}. 
  They are abundant and easy to detect, but are easily attenuated by matter which obstructs our view of their sources, especially source interiors.
  Cosmic rays are charged particles which have been detected with many techniques, but their trajectories are altered by magnetic fields, obscuring the true location of their production mechanisms~\citep{snowmass_cr}.
  Gravitational waves are elusive distortions of spacetime that travel at the speed of light. 
  They were only discovered within the past decade and have only been detected from very extreme and rare compact object mergers~\citep{snowmass_gw}.
  Neutrinos, however, have been observed for decades, are abundant at Earth, and they point back to their source due to their rare interactions with nature and can probe otherwise photon-opaque interiors.
  For example, studies of the interior of the Earth are possible using neutrino detectors~\citep{tomography_earth}.
  These astrophysical messengers originate from the same sources and cosmic ray interactions with matter may also produce neutrinos. 
  Therefore, the study of neutrinos directly contributes to the identification of sources of cosmic rays.
  Neutrino observations, especially at ultra-high energies, can also contribute to studies of neutrino-nucleon cross section among other topics~\citep{snowmass}.

  Cosmic rays (CRs) have been studied since the early 1900s. 
  Early twentieth century studies assumed the radiation observed on Earth came from the Earth itself, however Victor Hess launched a series of balloon-based experiments and found there was increasing radiation as the balloon rose, suggesting cosmic origin to the radiation~\citep{cr_hess}.
  We now know that there exists a flux of charged CRs generated by astrophysical objects that travel up to billions of light years to reach us.
  Figure~\ref{fig:CRspectrum} shows the cosmic ray spectrum observed at Earth~\citep{snowmass_cr}. 
  CRs affect the environment around Earth, for example, contributing to the aurora borealis and australis, corrupting electronics in satellites, and the radiation safety of astronauts in space. 
  Astronomers use CRs to further their understanding of the astronomical objects they originate from.  
  This thesis focuses on byproducts of Ultra-High Energy CRs (UHECRs), specifically UHECRs with $E\gtrsim 50$~EeV.
  These UHECRs are so energetic they can only be generated by the most extreme astrophysical objects, such as active galactic nuclei and compact object mergers which generate large magnetic fields and large outflows of particles.
  UHECR detector Pierre Auger reports that UHECRs with energies between 3 EeV and 100 EeV grow heavier in atomic number as energy increases~\citep{pao_mass_composition}.
  This is because lighter nuclei may interact with Extragalactic Background Light and the Cosmic Microwave Background in a process known as the Greisen-Zatsepin-Kuzmin (GZK) mechanism~\citep{gzk-gk, gzk-z} when the energy of a proton in the atomic nucleus exceeds 50~EeV.

  \noindent In total, one UHECR impacting on a cosmological photon can create 3 UHE neutrinos. 
  As such, this neutrino production channel enables UHE neutrino observations to not only further the study the sources of astrophysical neutrinos but also to constrain the flux and atomic composition of UHECRs. 
  As the sources of UHECRs are very powerful and rare, we expect them to mostly be extragalactic in origin. 
  UHECRs will be subjected to many magnetic fields that will deflect the UHECRs' trajectories on their way to Earth, obscuring the original source.
  UHE neutrino observations can therefore contribute to the study of UHECR sources.
  Like the UHECRs, UHE neutrinos are expected to travel cosmological distances before arriving at Earth and, as such, the flavor ratio of the UHE neutrino flux is expected to be equally electron, muon, and tau-flavored. 
  Unfortunately, both cosmic rays and neutrinos in this energy regime are expected to interact with Earth once per year per square kilometer, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:CRspectrum}, making them quite difficult to detect.

  Observations of UHE neutrinos also further our understanding of particle physics.
  The cross section of UHE neutrinos is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:neutrinocrosssection} and is only measured up to neutrino energies of 10~PeV.
  While particle colliders like the Large Hadron Collider can create particle collisions with a center of mass up to 100~PeV, current colliders can only observe neutrinos up to GeV energies~\citep{snowmass_neutrinofrontier}.
  There are many more applications of neutrino observations but these three (neutrino-nucleon cross section, astrophysical source studies, and UHECR applications) are the ones most relevant to this thesis.

  It follows from the large neutrino-nucleon cross section of UHE neutrinos, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:neutrinocrosssection}, that when these neutrinos reach Earth they should interact within the first $\mathcal{O}$(km) of Earth's crust.
  The interactions of neutrinos with matter are mediated by $Z$ and $W$ bosons, producing neutral-current (NC) and charged-current (CC) interactions, respectively. 
  In NC interactions, the neutrino exchanges a $Z^0$ boson with a quark in a nucleon, transferring momentum and energy into the quark that exceeds the binding energy of the atomic nucleus, propelling protons and neutrons forward in the ice.

  \noindent The quark is converted from an up quark into a down quark, converting the nucleon from a proton into a neutron. 
  Like in the NC interaction, this neutron has been imparted with so much momentum that it initiates a hadronic cascade. 
  The behavior of the outgoing lepton depends on the original neutrino's flavor. 
  Outgoing electrons will immediately interact with surrounding matter, initiating an electromagnetic cascade of mostly Bremsstrahlung and pair-production processes. 
  Outgoing muons and taus also interact with surrounding matter but have longer interaction and decay lengths, allowing them to travel some distance, stochastically deposit momentum into their environment, and continue traveling. 
  Taus have decay lengths $\mathcal{O}$(10~km) at ultra-high energies, mediated predominantly by the $W^{+/-}$ boson again, converting the tau back into a tau neutrino and producing hadrons or a pair of neutral and charged leptons like with the muon decay.
  If a pair of electron flavored leptons is generated, the outgoing electron or position immediately initiates an electromagnetic cascade. 
  If a pair of muon or tau leptons is generated, the outgoing taus, muons, and neutrinos continue to propagate.

  These highly energetic cascades are usually a few meters long and a couple centimeters in width. 
  In these showers, there is considerable pair production generating electrons and positrons. 
  For showers generated in dense dielectric media, such as ice, many positrons annihilate with electrons in the orbitals of nearby atoms.

  This means that the cascade develops an overall charge asymmetry and behaves as a rod of moving charge. 
  % \comment{Add EM derivations.} 
  As a charge in motion generates an electromagnetic field, so does the cascade.
  Due to the rod-like geometry of the cascade, wavelengths shorter than the extent of the rod deconstructively interfere while wavelengths longer than the cascade add coherently along a cone with an opening angle equal to the Cherenkov angle in the medium, 
  \begin{equation}
    \theta_C = \sin^{-1}(\frac{c}{nv})
    \label{eq:cherenkovangle}
  \end{equation}
  where $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum, $n$ is the refractive index of the medium, and $v$ is the speed of the particle emitting radiation.
  As most of the particles in a UHE cascade move near the speed of light $v\approx c$, simplifying the equation.
  As Gurgen Askaryan first theorized this phenomenon, the production of radiation created by the charge anisotropy of a particle cascade and propagated along a cone defined by the Cherenkov angle is called Askaryan Radiation~\citep{Askaryan_1961, Askaryan_1965}.

  Askaryan Radiation has a few important features of note including cone width, frequency spectra, and polarization.
  Ice is the dielectric medium used to monitor for UHE neutrino interactions in this thesis and the expected Askaryan Radiation is emitted in the radio-wavelengths 100~MHz-1~GHz, motivating the surveillance of ice with radio antennas. 
  Due to the long extent of UHE particle cascades, the Askaryan radiation exists in a narrow band around the emission cone. 
  If a radio antenna is near a UHE particle cascade but is not located along this cone, the Askaryan Radiation will be not be observed.
  In fact, the radiation begins to deteriorate if an antenna exists just a couple degrees off of the emission cone, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:askaryancone} where signal on-cone is impulsive and strong but drops dramatically with each degree the viewing angle is away from the Askaryan cone \citep{barwick_glaser_review}.
  The width of this cone of radiation is also larger for emission around 300~MHz compared to 1~GHz and for lower energy particle cascades, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:askaryanconewidth}.
  Another important feature of Askaryan Radiation is that it is polarized perpendicularly to the emission cone, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:askaryan_radiation}. 
  If observers of Askaryan Radiation can reconstruct the radiation's polarization, the geometry of the emission Cone can be reconstructed, and the direction of the cascade (and therefore, the direction of the original UHE neutrino) can be reconstructed. 

  While UHE neutrinos have not yet been observed using Askaryan Radiation, Askaryan emission from UHECR showers have been observed in ice by multiple experiments\citep{ara_cr, anita_cr, arianna_cr}.
  % and an outgoing muon from a $\sim220$PeV neutrino has been observed by KM3NeT via Cherenkov Radiation in the Mediterranean Sea~\citep{km3net_neutrino}.
  Particle cascades can develop in other dielectric media and emit Askaryan Radiation as well. 
  For example, Pierre Auger \citep{pierre_auger_uheneutrino}, BEACON \citep{BEACON}, TAMBO \citep{TAMBO}, ANITA \citep{anita_iv}, LOFAR~\citep{LOFAR}, and \mbox{RNO-G}~\citep{rnog_air_cr} look for Askaryan Radiation from in-air particle cascades (so-called ``air showers''). 
  However, only ice will be discussed going forwards.

  Ice is an abundant resource on Earth and is promising for the observations of Askaryan Radiation from UHE neutrino interactions given long absorption and scattering lengths of radio waves in this medium, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:attenuation_glaciers}. % cite?
  In 2006, as a proof of concept, Askaryan Radiation was observed experimentally for the first time by directing the beamline at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center into a 7.5 metric tons ice target~\citep{slac}. 
  Since then, many experiments have been designed to surveil ice from within, from the surface of, and above glaciers in the hopes of observing UHE neutrinos.

  Ice sheets on Greenland and Antarctica have been used to search for UHE neutrinos near stations established by the National Science Foundation. 
  In Greenland, detectors are built onto and into the 3000~m thick glacier near Summit Station. 
  In Antarctica, detectors are built on the 500~m thick Ross Ice Shelf near McMurdo Base, launched on balloons over the Antarctic glacier from the Long Duration Balloon facility also near McMurdo Base, and installed onto and into the 2800~m thick glacier near South Pole Station. 
  The ice comprising these glaciers naturally changes as a function of glacier depth leading radio-wavelegth emission to bend, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:raybending}. 
  This means that radiation emitted from different parts of the emission cone may travel different paths to arrive at the antenna. 
  We call these paths ``rays" and typically expect one ray to travel straight from the neutrino interaction to an antenna, while the second ray  may reflect off the surface of the ice or be overturned by the refractive properties of the ice.

  Glacial neutrino observatories use a variety of radio antennas, most shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Antenna-types}. 
  As some neutrino observatories are installed in narrow holes in the ice, we use antennas with less than 0.4~m in diameter. 
  To reconstruct the polarization of the signal, vertically polarized bicone antennas are used along with horizontally polarized quadslot antennas.%\footnote{\comment{One of my readers is confused what ``quadslot'' means. It seems from the testbed paper that at least ``bicone'' is jargon.?}}.
  For shallow and surface stations, Logarithmically Polarized Dipole Antennas are used. 
  These are sensitive to one polarization but multiple are typically installed, oriented in a variety of directions for signal polarization reconstruction.
  Lastly, dually polarized horn antennas are used on balloon-based neutrino experiments, wrapping around the entire balloon payload.

    The Askaryan Radio Array (ARA) is a collection of 5 stations spaced 2~km from each other near the South Pole where 4 holes per station are drilled up to 200~m deep and instrumented with radio antennas, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARA}~\citep{ara_design}. 
    ARA's first station was installed in the 2011/12 Austral summer season, the next two stations were installed in the 2012/13 season, and the last two were installed in the 2017/18 season.
    ARA has published analyses of solar flares \citep{ara_solarflare}, neutrino searches in data taken by 3 stations \citep{ara_a23, ara_pa_analysis}, and the first detection of naturally produced Askaryan Radiation in ice using cosmic rays~\citep{ara_cr}.
    The last ARA station was built with an extra phased array string with densely packed antennas, shown in red in Figure~\ref{fig:ARA}, that improves the efficiency of detecting low power signals using beamforming techniques~\citep{ara_pa_design, phased-array-concept}.
    ARA's dataset is the largest collection of in-ice neutrino interaction surveillance - exceptional for its extensive 15+ years of livetime and extended operation during periods of low background emissions. 
    The analysis and characterization of ARA data taken on all 5 stations from 2013 to 2023 is the focus of this thesis.

    The Radio Neutrino Observatory of Greenland (\mbox{RNO-G}) combines inspiration from ARIANNA's surface components and ARA's in-ice phased array into a next-generation hybrid detector, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rnog}~\citep{rnog_design}. 
    \mbox{RNO-G} has installed 8 of the planned 35 stations between 2021 and 2023 so far.
    \mbox{RNO-G} has published analyses of airplane transit reconstruction, solar flare reconstruction, and a cosmic ray air shower search using shallow antenna observations. 
    
    The next generation in-ice UHE neutrino detector is the radio extension of IceCube-Gen2~\citep{icecube_gen2}. 
    IceCube is an in-ice neutrino detector looking for Cherenkov light from neutrinos and finished construction in 2012.
    In the proposed expansion, IceCube-Gen2 will include hundreds of radio stations deployed near South Pole station and will increase IceCube's sensitivity to UHE neutrinos.
    Both ARIANNA-like surface stations and \mbox{RNO-G}-like stations with surface and deep-ice components are being considered for this radio array.

    A method for observing a greater volume of ice is to mount radio antennas to an atmospheric balloon and fly it over a glacier. 
    These detectors are both sensitive to Askaryan emission generated in ice that exits the Antarctic ice sheet and to tau air showers from earth skimming neutrino interactions, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:pueo}. 
    One downside is that the weather balloon often only flies for a couple of weeks before running out of the air needed to stay aloft. % Check this duration
    The gain in observation volume far outweighs the limited flight duration and has led to many successful flights and interesting results.
    The first experiment to mount radio antennas to a balloon was the ANITA collaboration who operated 4 flights~\citep{anita_iv}. 
    Multiple flights reconstructed signals from events that appear to have come from the Earth at angles that neutrinos are not expected to arrive from due to Earth absorption of UHE neutrinos~\citep{anita_anomalous1, anita_anomalous2}.
    Multiple collaborations have conducted followup searches for these events including the IceCube Neutrino Observatory~\citep{icecube_search_anita} and the Pierre Auger Observatory~\citep{pao_search_anita}.
    The ANITA flights have inspired the PUEO balloon which launched its first flight in 2025 and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pueo}~\citep{pueo_wp}.
  
  \subsection{In-Ice RADAR Detection}
  
    Another potential detection method for finding in-ice particle cascades related to UHE neutrinos does not involve observing radiation directly from the particle cascade, but by observing the cascade with RADAR.
    This is the design of the Radar Echo Telescope (RET) which was deployed near Summit Station in Greenland after testing the detection method at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center~\citep{ret_slac}. 
    A prototype RET station dedicated for in ice cosmic ray detection was deployed in the summer of 2023, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ret}, and consists of 
    \begin{itemize}
      \item One 8-antenna phased array transmitting string centered 10~m in the ice
      \item 3 receiving strings 30~m from the transmitter and equidistant from each other 
      \item 3 scintillator panels used to identify cosmic rays as they begin their cascades in air before impacting the ice~\citep{retcr_initialperformance} 
    \end{itemize}
    A cosmic ray search through RET data taken in 2024 was still ongoing during the summer of 2026~\citep{ret_cr_study}. 
    A neutrino-focused RET detector is still under development but proposals include a transmitting antenna buried deep in the ice with receiver antennas deployed into 9 boreholes drilled along a circle 200~m from the transmitter, also shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ret}~\citep{retn}.

\section{Thesis Overview}

  This thesis comprises simulations and analyses dedicated to finding UHE neutrinos using in-ice detectors at the South Pole and Greenland. 
  This chapter introduces the field of UHE neutrino observations and its place in multi-messenger astrophysics. 
  Next, Chapter~\ref{chap:ARA} describes the Askaryan Radio Array (ARA) and a literature review of previous results from the ARA collaboration is presented.
  The following Chapter~\ref{chap:veff} details simulation studies of ARA's sensitivity to UHE neutrinos, featuring the simulation of outgoing particles so that events like KM3NeT's UHE neutrino are considered in ARA's performance and characterized for potential observations. 
  Chapter~\ref{chap:5sa_cleaning} and Chapter~\ref{chap:5sa_analysis} of this thesis describe the cleaning and analysis of all ARA data taken from 2013 to 2023, resulting in a leading sensitivity on UHE neutrinos.
  A glossary of analysis variables and how they are constructed is presented in Appendix~\ref{app:5savariables}. 
  The analysis variables of the event discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:5sa_analysis} can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:pa_passingevent}.
  The thesis then concludes with Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizing all discussed topics and presenting next steps and suggestions for future detectors and UHE neutrino analyses, especially in the context of the Radio Neutrino Observatory of Greenland and IceCube-Gen2 Radio \citep{rnog_design, icecube_gen2}. 

  There are three more appendices covering topics I studied in my graduate career.
  Appendix~\ref{app:spacers} documents plastic centralizers designed to keep antennas centered in boreholes for the Radio Neutrino Observatory of Greenland. 
  Next, a simulation study is presented in Appendix~\ref{chap:gen2_infill} quantifying the benefits of joint Askaryan and Cherenkov emission observations in the proposed expansion of the IceCube Neutrino Observatory by placing radio antennas above planned optical modules. 
  Lastly, Appendix~\ref{app:AraSim} is the formal documentation of the AraSim simulation package written for \citep{ara_veff}.
