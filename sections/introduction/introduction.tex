\section{Ultra-High Energy Neutrinos and Cosmic Rays}

  Neutrinos are neutral leptons, nearly massless, rarely interact, and exist in 3 flavors akin to their charged lepton counterparts: the electron, muon, and tau flavors.
  Neutrinos are one of four multi-messengers and are a unique probe of astrophysical objects due to their abundance and rare interactions.
  Gravitational waves are elusive and have only been detected from very extreme and rare compact object mergers.
  Photons (i.e. radio waves, optical light, X-rays, and gamma-rays) are very abundant but easily attenuated by matter obstructing our view of their sources.
  Cosmic rays are also abundant but their paths are bent by magnetic fields which weakens their source's location to observers.
  Neutrinos, however, are abundant at Earth and point back to their source.

  Cosmic rays (CRs) have been studied since the early 1900s. 
  Scientists were observing radiation on Earth and assumed it came from the Earth itself, so Victor Hess launched a balloon-based experiment and found there was increasing radiation as the balloon rose, contrary to his theory.
  We now know that there exists a flux of charged CRs generated by astrophysical objects that travel up to billions of light years to reach us. 
  CRs affect every-day life around Earth, for example, contributing aurora borealis and australis, corrupting electronics in satellites, and the radiation safety of astronauts in space. 
  Astronomers also use CRs to further their understanding of the astronomical objects they originate from.  
  This thesis focuses on byproducts of Ultra-high energy CRs (UHECRs, energy>$10^{18}$~eV).
  UHECRs are so energetic they can only be generated by the most extreme astrophysical objects, for example active galactic nuclei and compact object mergers which generate large magnetic fields and large outflows of particles. 
  In the energy spectrum of hadronic CRs, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:CRspectrum}, there is an obvious increase of CRs around $3 \times 10^{18}$~eV. 
  This feature is colloquially referred to as ``the ankle'' of the CR spectrum.
  It is assumed that the sudden increase in CR flux above the ankle is driven by a transition from a Milky Way driven CR flux to an extragalactically driven one.
  Part of this is because our Milky Way galaxy does not point any UHECR generators in our direction and because the magnetic field encapsulating our galaxy is not strong enough to contain them.
  Another significant reason behind the ankle is that UHECRs with energies above $4 \times 10^{19}$~eV are likely to interact with photons comprising the Cosmic Microwave Background via the $\Delta^{\star}$-resonance, a process known as the Greisen-Zatsepin-Kuzmin (GZK). 
  The resulting $\Delta$ particles have a very short lifetime, decaying quickly into pions and hadrons.
  Charged pions also have short lifetimes, decaying most commonly into muons and muon-flavored neutrinos leading to an increase in UHE neutrinos. 
  As neutrinos are able to change flavors and extragalactically-generated neutrinos have plenty of time to oscillate, we expect UHECR neutrinos to arrive at earth in an equal mix between electron, muon, and tau flavors.
  Unfortunately, both cosmic rays and neutrinos in this energy regime are expected to interact with Earth once per year per square kilometer, making them quite rare.
  Regardless, this neutrino production channel enables UHE neutrino searches to not only further the study of astrophysical objects but also UHECRs.

  Observations of UHE neutrinos also further our understanding of particle physics.
  The cross section of UHE neutrinos is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:neutrinocrosssection} and is only measured up to neutrino energies of $10^7$~GeV.
  The Large Hadron Collider can only generate neutrinos up to energies of $10^8$~GeV, so our only source of UHE neutrinos is astrophysical and cosmogenic until larger colliders are built.
  There are many more applications of neutrino observations but these three (neutrino-nucleon cross section, astrophysical source studies, and UHECR applications) are the ones most relevant to this thesis.

  It follows from the large neutrino-nucleon cross section of UHE neutrinos as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:neutrinocrosssection}, that when UHE neutrinos reach Earth they should interact within the first $\mathcal{O}$~(km) of Earth's crust.
  The interactions of neutrinos with matter are mediated by $Z$ and $W$ bosons, producing neutral-current (NC) and charged-current (CC) interactions, respectively. 
  In NC interactions, the neutrino exchanges a $Z^0$ boson with a quark in a nucleon, transferring momentum and energy into the quark and splitting the nucleon.
  The original neutrino leaves the interaction (minus the energy that was transferred) while the fragmented nucleon, which now has very high momentum, begins colliding with nearby nuclei.

  The process of one nucleon imparting momentum on another nucleon, tearing this apart into multiple additional high energy nucleons, and repeating until resulting nucleons are no longer sufficiently energetic to continue, is called a hadronic cascade.
  In CC interactions, the neutrino exchanges a $W^{+/-}$ boson with a quark in a nucleon, transferring electric charge towards the neutrino which converts it into from a neutral lepton (a neutrino) into a charged lepton (an electron, muon, or tau particle). 
  The remaining quark is now neutral and, like with the NC interaction, has been imparted with so much momentum that it initiates a hadronic cascade. 
  The behavior of the outgoing lepton depends on its flavor. 
  Outgoing electrons will immediately interact with surrounding matter, initiating an electromagnetic cascade of mostly bremsstrahlung and pair-production processes. 
  Outgoing muons and taus also interact with surrounding matter but have longer interaction and decay lengths, allowing them to travel some distance, stochastically deposit momentum into their environment, and continue traveling with slightly less energy. 
  Taus have decay lengths $\mathcal(O)$~(10~km) at ultra-high energies, mediated predominantly by the $W^{+/-}$ boson again, converting the tau back into a tau neutrino and producing an additional pair of charged and neutral leptons.
  If a pair of electron flavored leptons is generated, the outgoing electron or position immediately initiates an electromagnetic cascade. 
  If a pair of muon or tau leptons is generated, the outgoing taus, muons, and neutrinos continue to propagate.

  These highly energetic cascades are usually a few meters long and a couple centimeters in width. 
  In these showers, there is a lot of pair production generating electrons and positrons. 
  For showers generated in dense dielectric media, many positrons annihilate with electrons in the orbitals of nearby atoms.

  This means that the cascade develops an overall charge asymmetry and is now essentially a rod of moving charge. 
  As a charge in motion generates an electromagnetic field, so does the cascade.
  Due to the rod-like geometry of the cascade, wavelengths shorter than the extent of the rod deconstructively interfere and cancel out while wavelengths longer than the cascade add coherently along a cone with an opening angle which is equal to the Cherenkov angle in the medium, 
  \begin{equation}
    \theta_C = \sin^{-1}(\frac{c}{nv})
    \label{eq:cherenkovangle}
  \end{equation}
  where $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum, $n$ is the refractive index of the medium, and $v$ is the speed of the particle emitting radiation.
  As most of the particles in a UHE cascade move near the speed of light, $v\approx c$, simplifying the equation.
  As Gurgen Askaryan first theorized this phenomenon, the production of radiation  created by the charge anisotropy of a particle cascade along the Cherenkov cone is called Askaryan Radiation~\cite{askaryan_radiation}. 

  Askaryan Radiation has a few important features of note.
  Ice is the dielectric medium used to monitor for UHE neutrino interactions in this thesis and the expected Askaryan Radiation is emitted in the radio-wavelengths 100~MHz-1~GHz, motivating the surveillance of ice with radio antennas. 
  Due to the long extent of UHE particle cascades, the Askaryan radiation exists in a narrow band around the Cherenkov cone. 
  If a radio antenna is near a UHE particle cascade but is not located on this cone, the Askaryan Radiation will be missed.
  In fact the radiation begins to deteriorate if an antenna exists just a couple degrees off of the Cherenkov cone, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:askaryancone} where signal on-cone is impulsive and strong but drops over 20 times when observed 1 degree away from the Cherenkov Cone and over 50 times when observed 2 degrees off-cone \cite{barwick_glaser_review}.
  Another important feature of Askaryan Radiation is that it is polarized perpendicular to the Cherenkov cone, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:askaryanradiationpolarization}. 
  If observers of Askaryan Radiation can reconstruct the radiation's polarization, the geometry of the Cherenkov Cone can be reconstructed, and the direction of the cascade (and therefore, the direction of the original UHE neutrino) can be reconstructed.

  While UHE neutrinos have not yet been observed using Askaryan Radiation, UHECRs have been observed in ice by the ARA experiment \cite{ara_cr}.
  The ARA experiment is the focus of this thesis, but the history of Askaryan Radiation observations using ice will be discussed in the coming sections.
  Particle cascades can develop in other dielectric media and emit Askaryan Radiation -- for example, Pierre Auger \cite{pierre_auger_uheneutrino}, BEACON \cite{BEACON}, TAMBO \cite{TAMBO}, ANITA \cite{anita_iv}, LOFAR~\cite{LOFAR}, and RNO-G~\cite{RNOG_air_cr} look for Askaryan Radiation from in-air particle cascades (so-called ``air showers'') -- but only ice will be discussed going forwards.

\section{First Generation Experiments and Results}

  While Gurgen Askaryan first published his theory of Askaryan Radiation in 1962, it wasn't experimentally detected until decades later. 
  In 2006, Askaryan Radiation was generated by directing the beamline at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center to impact onto 7.5 metric tons of ice and was successfully observed for the first time from ice using radio antennas placed above the ice\cite{slac_ice}.
