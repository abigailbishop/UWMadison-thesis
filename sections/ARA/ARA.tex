\chapter{The Askaryan Radio Array \label{chap:ARA}}

    As briefly discussed in Section~\ref{sec:iniceExperiments}, the Askaryan Radio Array (ARA) is an experiment searching for UHE neutrinos at the South Pole.
    The array consists of 5 stations, each with 4-5 strings instrumented with radio antennas monitoring for Askaryan Radiation.
    This chapter expands on ARA's design and layout, operations, and summarizes a selection of previous analyses. 

\section{Station Design and Layout \label{sec:design_layout}}

    The station layout of ARA is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ARA}; the 5 stations are arranged on a hexagonal grid with 2~km spacing near the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and a couple kilometers from the South Pole. 
    For ARA, the 2~km station spacing was chosen to balance full array volume, observations of the same neutrino event in multiple stations, and the cost of equipment and installation~\citep{ara_design}.

        For each antenna, an arriving radio signal is transformed from an electric field into a voltage trace according to the antenna's response function.
        Deep in the borehole, the trace is passed through a notch filter that suppresses frequencies around 450~MHz, where most South Pole communications frequencies are transmitted.
        Next, the trace is amplified with a Low Noise Amplifier (LNA) that is located near the antenna. 
        Following this, the trace is converted into an optical signal and sent to the data acquisition (DAQ) box on the surface. 
        We expect that the LNA's occasionally experience power surges after discovering a class of events with clear impulses in some channels and high root-mean-square waveform values in some antennas. 
        The reconstructions of these events coincide with downhole antennas and LNAs, though this has not been experimentally verified and we do not currently know the cause of these sparks.

        Each station also has one or two additional strings with radio antennas, called Calibration Pulsers (CPs), that pulse radio waves at a rate of 1~Hz. 
        CP events are used to monitor the performance of ARA stations over time and have occasionally alerted collaborators to adjust station settings to improve observations.
        Only one CP is active during each run and Tab.~\ref{tab:A2cp_runs} documents which CP was active during each run. 

        All cables from each data taking and CP string are directed into a large, roughly 1~m by 2.5~m, box called a vault. 
        The vault contains a network switch, a power box, a DAQ box, and Fiber Optic Amplification Modules that convert the optical signals from each data taking string into electronics signals before they enter the DAQ box\footnote{\comment{Check this.         As roughly 28 centimeters of snow accumulates at the South Pole per year (mostly due to snow drift, not precipitation) \citep{sp_snow_accumulation}, these vaults become buried overtime.
        Vault extensions (large empty wooden boxes with a ladder) can be installed over the existing vault to improve future accessibility.

        DAQ boxes are the nexus of station data taking and control. 
        The sides of the boxes are made of solid aluminum about 1~cm thick, for additional radio-frequency shielding, and are 10-40~cm along each side.
        Like the vault, there is a network switch, a power board, and ports that connect to each antenna and calibration pulser for the station. 
        The main piece of electronics in the DAQ box is called the ARA Triggering and Readout Interface (ATRI) board which includes a single-board-computer (SBC), an Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA, a customizable logic device often used in custom electronics), and stacks of triggering and digitizing boards that connect to each antenna~\citep{ara_design}.
        The predecessor to the ATRI board was the ICRR board and was used on A1 until the 2013-14 South Pole Season when it was replaced with an ATRI board.
        A station's SBC is what technicians connect to when accessing each station. 
        They contain and run scripts that program the FPGA for data taking and calibration pulsing, recording data, and packaging data for retrieval by processes on the Radproc computer.

        In the DAQ box, the optical signals from the strings are converted back into voltage traces and then passed through band filters which suppress out of band noise. % Above 1GHz and below 1 MHz right?
        The voltage is then split and passed simultaneously through a Triggering Daughter Array (TDA) and a Digitizing Daughter Array (DDA). 
        Before entering the TDA, the traces pass through tunnel diodes that convert voltage into power. 
        The TDA then analyzes if three antennas of the same polarization observed a signal with more than $\sim 6$ times the power of noise.
        If this occurs, the signal that was simultaneously digitized by the DDA is saved to the local Single Board Computer (SBC).
        This electronics chain is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARA-signalchain}.
        Each TDA and DDA have four inputs and are arranged to take in signals from the four antennas on each string. 
        Occasionally, the DDA will experience a power surge and amplify the signal in one string. 
        We refer to these as spark events and they are typically excluded from analyses.

        This chip was installed on ATRI's used for stations A1-A5 between 2013 and 2018.

        ARA data is saved in batches, called ``runs'', typically taken over the course of 3 hours.
        There are three types of events: forced/software events, RF events, and events. 
        Software triggered events read out $\sim200$~ns of ambient noise waveforms at a rate of $\sim 1$~Hz and are used to build simulations and define station livetime configurations (discussed in Section~\ref{sec:a2configs}).
        RF (``Radio Frequency") events are the main event class and the one that we search for neutrino observations.
        An RF trigger occurs on if three antennas of the same polarization observe waveforms with power $\sim 6$ times more than the thermal background.
        Lastly, calibration pulser (CP) events are recorded via the RF event trigger but are classified as CP events by the DAQ board if the CP was firing at the same time the RF event was read out.
        At a combined trigger rate (software events + RF events + CP events) of $\sim11$~Hz, each run typically contains 100-120 thousand events. 
        Between each run, the station will enter into a brief state of lower power consumption as data is not being taken and the calibration pulser is not firing. 
        For, conservatively, the first minute of each run, as currents rise back to a nominal level, data taken by the detector is unusual and therefore typically excluded from analyses. 

    \subsection{Phased Array}

      Between the 4 traditional strings of A5 is another string, also shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ARA}, instrumented with 7 vertically polarized antennas installed between depths of 173~m and 180~m~\citep{ara_pa_design}.
      This type of instrument, called a Phased Array (PA), uses a beamforming trigger as was proposed for radio observations of neutrinos by \citet{phased-array-concept}.
      Beamforming triggers use predetermined anticipated signal arrival directions\footnote{\comment{Why are these arrival directions typically symmetric around an elevation of 0? Wouldn't we want to skew these towards deep ice, where we expect to find the most neutrinos?}} to calculate the time delay between the arrival of such signals between pairs of antennas and automatically shifts the observed signals according to these delays\footnote{\comment{I want to find or make a figure showing how these signals add up and decrease SNR. Could also refer to the CSW explanation in the appendix if I add enough to it.}}.
      Once the waveforms are shifted, they are summed together, amplifying coherent and impulsive signals.
      This magnifies low power signals that may not have been observed from the waveforms alone.
      This PA string has a DAQ board separate from A5's and two horizontally polarized antennas placed below the 7 vertically polarized antennas to assist in event reconstruction.
      When the PA triggers, the A5 detector will force the readout of an event so that the 16 A5 antennas can also enhance event reconstruction.
      When A5 and the PA were first installed, they were completely separate besides the outrigger pathway that forces A5 to readout when the PA triggers. 
      In the season of 2018-19, the readout of one of the vertically polarized antennas on A5 was split so that it could be read out by A5 and the PA. 
      This allows analyzers to synchronize the clocks between the two detectors, improving joint A5 and PA event reconstruction.
      In October 2019 the A5 DAQ board suffered the FX2 USB failure and stopped taking data. 
      With 6 inputs available on the PA DAQ board, the readout of 6 vertically polarized antennas from A5 were connected to the PA DAQ in the season of 2019-20.
      The 7 total A5 antennas connected to the PA DAQ board do not contribute to triggering but do aid in event reconstruction.

\section{Operations Pipeline}

    \subsection{Data Transfer Pipeline}
  
        The runs of data that are saved by each station are collected by the radio processing (Radproc) computer which is housed in the IceCube Laboratory (ICL). 
        This data transfer and any other communications with the stations is conducted over fiber optic cable buried in the ice. 
        These fiber optic cables pass through a series of patch panels to reach their destination. 
        Starting at Radproc, information travels over intranet to a White Rabbit Switch\footnote{\url{https://www.white-rabbit.tech/wr-technology}{https://www.white-rabbit.tech/wr-technology}}, a network switch used to improve time synchronization between connected devices. 
        Ethernet leaves the White Rabbit Switch and is converted by a Small Form-factor Pluggable (SFP) into an optical signal that then enters a fiber optic patch panel in the ICL. 
        Outgoing fiber optic cables exit the ICL and run down the West Tower into the West Tower Junction Box, where the second patch panel is and leads immediately to a third patch panel located in the same box. 
        Cables leaving the third patch panel are laid over 1~km and end at Wind Turbine 3 where they meet their fourth patch panel. 
        Bundles of fiber optic cables are then run to stations A1, A2, and A3. 
        At this point, 2 fiber optic cables in each bundle are connected to A1, A2, and A3 while the last 2 cables at A1 are connected to a switch directing fiber optic signal to A4 and the last 2 cables at A2 being connected to a switch directing signal to A5.

        ARA's power cabling follows a similar layout between the ICL and Wind Turbine 3. 
        Stations A1 and A4 are connected to the same power supply in the ICL, similar to the networking setup, however A2, A3, and A5 have their own power supplies\footnote{\comment{Are these separate power cables or does a power cable go into A1 and then a new cable connects A1 power to A4?}}.

        The Radproc computer in the ICL collects all the data from A1-A5 and the Phased Array.
        $\sim 1\%$ of events in each run are randomly selected, forming our monitoring data sample. 
        The monitoring data is put into a data ``dropbox" (i.e. a directory titled \texttt{dropboxes}) going ``north'' and the full run file is put into a data drop box going ``south''. 
        These drop boxes are coordinated by IceCube data specialists. 
        The data in the ``north'' drop box is sent over satellite to the University of Wisconsin Madison (UW--Madison) for processing and used in monitoring and operations. 
        This data sample is automatically processed by an ARA Operations lead so collaborators may generate and monitor event displays and station metadata (temperature, event rates, etc) on \href{http://aware.wipac.wisc.edu}{AWARE}.
        The data in the ``south'' drop box is stored on hard drives (also referred to as ``tapes'') that are accumulated and shipped at the end of each calendar year. 
        These hard drives arrive at UW--Madison in the spring and are uploaded to the IceCube data warehouse in late spring and early summer.
  
    \subsection{Data Processing}
  
        Data straight from the hard drives are referred to as ``raw'' or ``L0'' data. 
        With some packages, like \texttt{pyaradisplay}\footnote{Documented in \href{https://aradocs.wipac.wisc.edu/cgi-bin/DocDB/ShowDocument?docid=937}{https://aradocs.wipac.wisc.edu/cgi-bin/DocDB/ShowDocument?docid=937} but updated in 2024 by John Kelley, Alan Salcedo Gomez, and me.}, one can plot waveforms however this does not include any calibrations and is not quickly readable for analysis.
        Therefore, once uploaded, A1-A5 L0 data is transformed into a ROOT~\citep{root} readable, L1 format by the ARA Mass Processing\footnote{
          \href{https://github.com/AndrewShultz/AraMassProcessing}{https://github.com/AndrewShultz/AraMassProcessing}, written by Andrew Schultz.
        } package and PA data is transformed by the NuPhase Rootification\footnote{
          \href{https://github.com/vPhase/nuphase_rootification}{https://github.com/vPhase/nuphase\_rootification}, written by me, based on scripts by Kaeli Hughes and the \href{https://github.com/vPhase/nuphaseroot}{\texttt{NuPhaseRoot}} and \href{https://github.com/vPhase/libnuphase}{\texttt{libNuPhase}} packages that were created by Cosmin Deaconu. 
          Note that the \texttt{NuPhase Rootification} package is in a private repository that cannot be viewed by those who are not members of the \href{https://github.com/vPhase}{\texttt{vPhase}} GitHub organization.
        } package.
        The process of transforming L0 into L1 data is called ``rootification'' or ``unpacking''\footnote{IceCube IT refers to the uploading of L0 data to the IceCube data warehouse as ``unpacking'' so I refer to the transformation of L0 into L1 data as ``rootification''. ARA collaborators may still use ``unpacking'' to describe L1 generation.}. 
        Colleagues at the University of Nebraska Lincoln rootify A1-A5 data under the supervision of Ilya Kravchenko while I have rootified PA data with advice from Cosmin Deaconu and Marco Muzio. 
        Over the past year I trained Sanyukta Agarwal at Kansas University to rootify PA data. 

        After ARA data has been rootified, the data is validated. 
        Some runs are too small and are logged as such in presentations on the ARA Analysis Call and pages like \href{https://mediawiki.icecube.wisc.edu/ara/index.php/2020_L0-L1_Unpacking_Notes}{the 2020 L0-L1 Unpacking Notes}. 
        A series of validation tests are performed to assess that the data has been unpacked properly and to identify any bad runs. 
        Some validation metrics include analyzing the number of samples in a waveform, sampling rate, waveform root mean square, and repeated event numbers.
        
        After data has been rootified, data is split into two samples: a full 100\% sample and a smaller 10\% sample\footnote{
            The smaller A1-A5 sample is technically reduced to a 9.5\% sample, but we round this to 10\% when referring to it.
        }. 
        Data analyses are typically built on the 10\% data sample then applied to the 100\% sample, as a blinding strategy. 
        For an experiment looking for rare data in a band of electromagnetic frequencies that strongly overlaps with anthropogenic backgrounds (e.g. snowmobile spark plugs, radios, airplanes, weather balloons, etc), building an analysis on only 10\% of the data has proven risky. 
        For example, unpredicted backgrounds have survived final event selection in some analyses and other analyses have removed an excessive number of events to avoid surviving backgrounds, both reducing analysis impact. 
        It would benefit us to consider other blinding strategies in addition to this one.

\section{Previous Analyses} 

  ARA has been releasing technical design and analysis papers for over a decade. A selection of studies are discussed in this section: ice studies performed with ARA data, solar flare reconstruction, the observation of Askaryan Radiation from cosmic rays, and analyses with A2, A3, A5, and PA. 

\subsection{Ice Studies} 

  ARA has been used to study the ice around South Pole both to enhance our understanding of the array's environment and to contribute to the field of glaciology. 
  A recent publication used data emitted by a radio pulser deployed to depths between 700~m and 1300~m and favored modeling the South Pole ice as three exponential functions~\citep{ice_ara}.
  The simulation and analyses presented in this thesis, however, use a single exponential function \comment{Based on the one from the PA analysis but I think we changed something?}
  ARA observations have also been used to investigate the effect of birefringence on radio observations~\citep{ice2019}. 
  Radio signals traveling perpendicular to the flow of ice experience significant propagation delays but not when travelling parallel to ice flow. 
  The polarization of the radio signal may also change due to birefringence, affecting ARA's ability to reconstruct the astrophysical source of neutrino events and is an active area of investigation~\citep{ara_birefringence}.
  These studies use SPICE core data and A2 and A4 observations of radio waves from a pulser deployed into the SPICE core hole to estimate the rate at which signal polarization changes as radio waves travel between the SPICE core and the receiving station.

\subsection{Solar Flare Reconstruction} 
  Solar flares typically release large amounts of radio-wavelength radiation, making them a good source for calibrating the ARA detector. 
  On February 15, 2011, a solar flare released substantial radiation for 70 minutes that was visible to ARA's prototype station, the Testbed \citep{ara_solarflare}\footnote{\comment{Should I have mentioned the Testbed earlier?}}. 
  2,000 events that were recorded over these 70 minutes were highly correlated with the location of the Sun, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARAsolarflare}.
  The approximately 2 degree offset in reconstructed azimuth compared to expected azimuth is attributed to systematic uncertainties in the reconstruction algorithms that were used.
  This analysis not only demonstrated the Testbed's, and therefore ARA's, reliability in reconstructing signals from distant sources, but having a source with a known location helps the collaboration tune and improve the accuracy of our analysis methods.

  As discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro}, UHE Cosmic Ray (CR) particle showers initiated in Earth's atmosphere can impact upon the South Pole glacier. 
  The relative charge excess of such a particle shower is demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cr_ice}, showing an air shower that develops over $\sim 10$~km and a denser core in ice with a great increase in the observed charge excess confined to just $\sim 30$~m of the surface. 
  If a CR impacts within a few hundred meters of an ARA station, they may be observed by one of our stations. % Cite nicolas' thesis or a cr paper
  Previous neutrino searches have observed events containing signatures that could indicate radio-frequency radiation from a UHECR air shower that triggered our stations \citep{ara_a23, ara_pa_analysis}.  
  A recent publication from the ARA collaboration, led by Nat Alden and Philipp Windischhofer at the University of Chicago, deconvolved the electric fields of 13 highly impulsive and signal-like events with high reconstructed elevations originally observed by an analysis of A5 and PA data reported in \citet{ara_pa_analysis} and summarized in Section~\ref{sec:analysis_pa}~\citep{ara_cr}.
  The frequency content of the electric fields of these events are compared to simulated cosmic rays and established that the frequency content of the 7 data events were consistent with originating from a shower-like source in shallow ice, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cr_events}.
  This was the first in ice observation of Askaryan Radiation from cosmic particles and was a great advancement in our field. 
  Similar studies are currently being conducted using similar events from the \citet{ara_a23} publication (summarized in Section~\ref{sec:analysis_a23}) that reconstruct towards the surface~\citep{ara_cr_a2}.

  After 5 years of operation, the ARA collaboration carried out its first neutrino search. 
  Almost 2,200 days of combined livetime were analyzed from the second and third ARA station using a cut-based analysis\index{Cut-Based Analysis} \citep{ara_a23_10mo,ara_a23}. % The actual number is 2,162 days
  This type of analysis is built by defining a collection of criteria, called cuts, such that only a neutrino event will pass every cut, discriminating signal from background and contamination. 
  These criteria include detector and background-related qualifications that filter out events like known calibration pulsing events, events with software glitches, and runs with known or assumed anthropogenic signals. 
  After these events are removed, cuts are applied to remove cosmic ray contamination. 
  As these events take place above the ice and sometimes also in the first few meters of the firn, cosmic rays are expected only from fairly inclined directions and are excluded by removing all events from inclined directions. 
  For example, this analysis applied a cut removing all events with a elevation less than 60$^{\circ}$ in A2 and 80$^{\circ}$ in A3. 
  This is a fairly harsh cut that removes ``10-30\% of neutrinos" from simulated neutrino datasets \citep{ara_a23}, so improvements to this cut involving deliberate cosmic ray identification tools are an area of active development \citep{CRiniceARENA}. 
  After removing all background events that can be removed trivially, all remaining events are assumed to originate from thermal backgrounds or from real neutrino signal. 
  Signal is then identified by determining if an event's reconstruction confidence ($C_{\text{sky}}$, similar to the \texttt{max\_corr} variables in Appendix~\ref{app:5savariables}) and signal power (\texttt{root\_power\_ratio}, defined in Appendix~\ref{app:5savariables}) are both high enough to correspond to a neutrino, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARA-A23-SignalCut}.

  This analysis involved two pipelines with slightly different cuts and configurations. 
  All events that pass these cuts are assumed to be signal-like events, though they are scrutinized further to ensure all detections are neutrino events. 
  Neither pipeline found any neutrino-like events, though both found interesting events when all cuts were imposed on data except for the Zenith Cut. 
  One analysis found 2 surface-like events in A2 and 1 in A3 (in addition to 3 events that are thought to be anthropogenic in origin).
  The second found 7 ``near surface" events in A2 and the same A3 event found in the first analysis (with 2 more events from the same run as the anthropogenic triplet found in the first analysis, so they're assumed to be background as well). 
  At the time of publication, none of these surface events had been further analyzed to determine if they were cosmic ray events but are now under investigation as mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:analysis_cr}. 
  Some events that passed Analysis B of \citet{ara_a23} had ``an unphysical amount of power [that] was deposited in one or two strings''. 
  One of these events is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARA-A23-sparkevent}.
  These events were removed post-unblinding by employing a reconstruction algorithm based on hit times \comment{called AraVertex?}. 
  One event did not have sufficient signal for and the other three reconstructed into the surface region.

  An analysis was conducted on the 208.7 days of livetime in 2019 after the A5 channel output was split (to be read out by both A5 and PA DAQ boards) and before the A5 DAQ went down~\citep{ara_pa_analysis}.
  During this period, the clock on teh 2 DAQ boards could be synchronized using this shared channel, allowing for powerful joint A5+PA reconstruction of events. 
  A cut based analysis was used for this study as well, where cuts were designed over 10\% of the data to eliminate events of anthropogenic, cosmic ray, faulty detector, and other non-thermal and non-signal origin.
  After all cuts were chosen, tuned, and applied to 10\% of the data, all events in the 10\% sample that passed all cuts were assumed to be entirely composed of thermal background events. 
  Using these thermal background events and a database of simulated neutrino events, a Fischer Discriminant was trained to identify signal and thermal noise based events to use on the full 100\% dataset \citep{fisherDiscriminant}.
  46 events that passed the analysis and reconstructed in the ``surface region'' were discussed briefly, noting that this dataset may contain cosmic ray candidates which were later analyzed in \citet{ara_cr}.
  1 event passed the analysis in the ``deep region'' but did not appear impulsive enough to have come from a neutrino cascade, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:analysis_pa_passingevent}.
