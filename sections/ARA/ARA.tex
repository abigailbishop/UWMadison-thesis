\chapter{The Askaryan Radio Array}

    As briefly discussed in Section~\ref{sec:iniceExperiments}, the ARA is an experiment searching for UHE neutrinos at the South Pole.
    5 stations comprise the array where each station has at least 4 strings instrumented with radio antennas targeting Askaryan Radiation.
    This chapter expands on ARA's design and layout, operations, and summarizes previous analyses. 

\section{Station Design and Layout}

    The station layout of ARA was shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ARA}; the 5 stations were arranged on a hexagonal grid with 2~km spacing near the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and a couple kilometers from the South Pole. 
    For ARA, it was determined that building stations in a hexagonal grid with a spacing of 2 kilometers between stations was the right balance between neutrino detection, cost of equipment and installation, and the possibility to observe a single neutrino interaction with multiple detectors~\citep{ara_design}.

    Figure~\ref{fig:ARA} also showed an example of the A5 station.
    Each ARA station is designed to be a rectangular lattice of pairs of horizontally and vertically polarized quadslot and bicone antennas, respectively.
    This is done by drilling 4 holes in the ice arranged in a square and deploying two pairs of antennas into each hole, each pair separated in depth by 18-30~m. 
    The square layout of the hole has a horizontal baseline of 14-32~m.
    The specifics for each of the 5 stations is shown in Table~\ref{tab:station_lattices}. 

    In the center of the 4 holes, A5 has a fifth hole drilled to 200~m and instrumented with 7 vertically polarized antennas packed between depths of 173~m and 180~m that use a beamforming trigger to better detect low-signal events.
    This trigger method involves predetermining expected signal arrival delays between pairs of antennas, shifting observed signal according to these delays, and adding all shifted signal together. 
    From thermal noise, the signal observed is random and will result in a summed waveform resembling a noise waveform.
    Alternatively, an impulsive wavefront read out by multiple antennas will align after shifting all waveforms according to their time delays so the summation of signals from the waveform will add together and rise above the noise. 
    This detector is called the Phased Array and has a dedicated data acquisition board and two horizontally polarized antennas placed below the 7 vertically polarized antennas to assist in event reconstruction.

    Each station also has one or two additional strings with radio antennas, called Calibration Pulsers (CPs), that pulse roughly once per second. 
    The stations trigger on these radio pulses naturally (i.e. detector readout is not forced but instead is only read out if detector trigger logic is satisfied) but are tagged as a CP event if the CP was firing at the time of event readout.

    All cables from each data taking and CP string are directed into a large, roughly 1~m by 2.5~m, box called a vault. 
    The vault contains a network switch, a power box, a data acquisition (DAQ) box, Fiber Optic Amplification Modules that convert optical signals from each data taking string into electronics signals before they enter the DAQ box.
    These vaults have shielding to protect the detector from triggering on electronics in the vault.
    As roughly 28 centimeters of snow accumulates at the South Pole per year (mostly due to snow drift, not precipitation) \citep{sp_snow_accumulation}, these vaults become buried overtime.
    Vault extensions (large empty wooden boxes with a ladder) can be installed over the existing vault to improve future accessibility. 
    Figure~\ref{fig:a4vault} shows the electronics in the vault for Station A4 viewed from the edge of a vault extension.

        DAQ boxes are the nexus of station data taking and control. 
        The sides of the boxes are about 1~cm thick, 10-40~cm along each side, and made of solid aluminum, for additional radio-frequency shielding.
        Like the vault, there is a network switch, a power board, and ports that connect to each antenna and calibration pulser for the station. 
        The main piece of electronics is called the ARA Triggering and Readout Interface (ATRI) board which includes a single-board-computer (SBC), an FPGA, and stacks of triggering and digitizing boards that connect to each antenna.
        The predecessor to the ATRI board was the ICRR board and was used on A1 until the 2013-14 South Pole Season when it was replaced with an ATRI board.
        The Phased Array instrument has a dedicated ``NuPhase'' DAQ setup comprised of two 8-channel boards an external SBC. 
        A station's SBC is what technicians connect to when accessing each station. 
        They contain scripts that program the FPGA for data taking and calibration pulsing, recording data, and packaging data for retrieval by processes on the Radproc computer.
        ARA data is saved in batches, called ``runs'', over the course of 3 hours.
        At a combined trigger rate (CP events, forced events, and RF events) of $\sim11$~Hz, each run contains roughly 100-120 thousand events. 
        Between each run, the station will enter into a brief state of lower power consumption as data is not being taken and the calibration pulser is not firing. 
        For, conservatively, the first minute of each run, as currents rise back to a nominal level, data taken by the detector is unusual and therefore typically removed from analyses. 

        The chip enabling connection of the FPGA with the ATRI is called the FX2.
        This chip was installed on ATRI's used for stations A1-A5 between 2013 and 2018. 
        The USB connection between the FX2 and the ATRI degrades overtime preventing the SBC to communicate with the FPGA, rendering the station unable to take data.

        For each antenna, an arriving signal is transformed from an electric field into a voltage trace according to the antenna's response function.
        Deep in the borehole, the trace is passed through a notch filter that suppresses signal around 450~MHz, where most South Pole communications frequencies are set.
        Next, the trace is amplified with a Low Noise Amplifier (LNA). 
        Following this, the trace is converted into an optical signal and sent to the data acquisition box on the surface. 
        In the data acquisition box, the signal is converted back to a voltage trace and then passed through band filters which suppress out of band noise. % Above 1GHz and below 1 MHz right?
        The voltage is then split and passed simultaneously through a Triggering Daughter Array (TDA) and a Digitizing Daughter Array (DDA). 
        Before entering the TDA, the traces pass through tunnel diodes that convert voltage into power. 
        The TDA then analyzes if three antennas with the same polarization observed a signal with more than $\approx 6$ times the power of noise.
        If this occurs, the signal that was simultaneously digitized by the DDA is saved to the local Single Board Computer (SBC).
        This electronics chain is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARA-signalchain}.
        Each TDA and DDA have four inputs and are arranged to take in signals from the four antennas on each string. 
        Occasionally, the DDA will experience a power surge and amplify the signal in one string. 
        We refer to these as spark events and they are typically removed from analyses.

\section{Operations Pipeline}

    \subsection{Data Transfer Pipeline}
  
        The data that is saved by each station is collected by the radio processing (Radproc) computer which is housed in the IceCube Laboratory (ICL). 
        This data transfer and any other communications with the stations is conducted over fiber optic cable buried in the ice. 
        These fiber optics cables pass through a series of patch panels to reach their destination. 
        Starting at Radproc, ethernet signal is converted into optical signal and passed enters a patch panel in the ICL. 
        The fiber optics cables exit the ICL and run down the West Tower into the West Tower Junction box, where the second patch panel is. 
        Cables leaving the second patch panel are laid over 1~km and end at Wind Turbine 3 where they meet their third patch panel. 
        Bundles of fiber optics cables are then run to stations A1, A2, and A3. 
        At this point, 2 fiber optics cables in each bundle are connected to A1, A2, and A3 while the last 2 cables at A1 are directed into a switch directing signal to A4 and the last 2 cables at A2 being directed into a switch directing signal to A5. 
        The last 2 kilometers of fiber optics cable laid connect A1 to A4 and A2 to A5. 
        I compiled the mapping of all fibers from the ICL to ARA stations and the IceCube Surface Array into a spreadsheet that is available in \href{https://aradocs.wipac.wisc.edu/DocDB/DocumentDatabase}{ARA DocDB\#\#\#\#}. % todo not the real link

        Note that, in January 2025, we discovered the fibers connecting the ICL patch panel port 19 and 20 to the West Tower Junction Box patch panel were reversed compared to documentation and has been updated in the aforementioned DocDB article. 
        Handling of fibers in the West Tower Junction Box which connect the ICL to A3 via port 19 were handled unintentionally when installing the IceCube Surface Array extension. 
        Impurities may have been introduced into these fibers and the field team recommends retermination of the wires in the West Tower Junction Box in a future field season to improve network connection to A3.

        % Would be nice to have a diagram of the networking

        ARA's power cabling follows a similar layout between the ICL and Wind Turbine 3. 
        Stations A1 and A4 are connected to the same power supply in the ICL, similar to the networking setup, however A2, A3, and A5 have their own power supplies. 
        A2 and A3 used to share a power supply but drew too much power when being turned on after the power supplies experienced a full shut down.

        The Radproc computer in the ICL collects all the data from A1-A5 and the Phased Array then randomly selects $\approx 1\%$ of events that constitute our monitoring data. 
        The monitoring data is put into a data drop box going ``north'' and the full data file is put into a data drop box going ``south''. 
        These drop boxes are monitored by IceCube data specialists. 
        The data in the ``north'' drop box is sent over satellite to the University of Wisconsin Madison (UW Madison) for processing and use in monitoring and operations. 
        This data sample is automatically processed by an ARA Operations lead for use viewing event displays and station metadata (temperature, event rates, etc) on \href{http://aware.wipac.wisc.edu}{AWARE}.
        The data in the ``south'' drop box is stored on hard drives that are accumulated and shipped at the end of each calendar year, which arrive at UW Madison in the spring and are uploaded to the IceCube data warehouse in late spring and early summer.
  
    \subsection{Data Processing}
  
        Data arriving on hard drives (also referred to as ``tapes'') is referred to as ``raw'' or ``L0'' data and typically takes a few weeks to be uploaded to the IceCube data warehouse after the hard drives physically arrive in Madison.

        Once uploaded, L0 data is transformed into a ROOT~\citep{root} readable format by the ARA Mass Processing\footnote{
          \href{https://github.com/AndrewShultz/AraMassProcessing}{https://github.com/AndrewShultz/AraMassProcessing}, written by Andrew Schultz.
        } package  for data taken by A1-A5 data acquisition boards and by the NuPhase Rootification\footnote{
          \href{https://github.com/vPhase/nuphase_rootification}{https://github.com/vPhase/nuphase\_rootification}, written by me, based on scripts by Kaeli Hughes and the \href{https://github.com/vPhase/nuphaseroot}{\texttt{NuPhaseRoot}} and \href{https://github.com/vPhase/libnuphase}{\texttt{libNuPhase}} packages which were created by Cosmin Deaconu. 
          Note that the \texttt{NuPhase Rootification} package is in a private repository that cannot be viewed if one is not a member of the \href{https://github.com/vPhase}{\texttt{vPhase}} GitHub organization.
        } package for PA data in a process called ``rootification''. 
        Colleagues at the University of Nebraska Lincoln rootify A1-A5 data under the supervision of Ilya Kravchenko while I have rootified PA data with advice from Cosmin Deaconu and Marco Muzio. 

        Some runs are too small and some are just weird and are noted as bad at this stage. 

        After ARA data has been rootified, a series of tests are performed to assess that the data has been unpacked properly and all bad runs have been identified. 
        
        After data has been rootified, data is split into two samples: a full 100\% sample and a smaller 10\% sample\footnote{
            The smaller A1-A5 sample is technically reduced to a 9.5\% sample, but we typically round this to 10\% when referring to it.
        }. 
        Data analyses are typically built on the 10\% data sample then applied to the 100\% sample, as a blinding strategy. 
        For an experiment looking for rare data in an electromagnetic frequency band that strongly overlaps with anthropogenic backgrounds (e.g. snowmobiles, radios, airplanes, weather balloons, etc) that can appear unexpectedly at times, building an analysis on only 10\% of the data has proven risky. 
        For example, unprecedented backgrounds have survived to final event selection and other analyses have removed excessive number of events to avoid surviving backgrounds, both reducing an analysis' impact. 

\section{Previous Analyses} 

ARA has been releasing technical design and analysis papers for over a decade. A selection of studies are discussed in this section. 

\subsection{Solar Flare Reconstruction} 
Solar flares typically release large amounts of radio-wavelength radiation, making them a good source for calibrating the ARA detector. 
On February 15, 2011, a solar flare released substantial radiation for 70 minutes that was visible to ARA's prototype station, the Testbed \citep{ara_solarflare}. 
2,000 events were found over these 70 minutes that were highly correlated with the location of the Sun, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARAsolarflare}.
The approximately 2 degree offset in reconstructed azimuth compared to expected azimuth is attributed to systematic uncertainties in the reconstruction algorithms that were used.
This analysis not only demonstrated the Testbed's, and therefore the ARA detector's, reliability in reconstructing signals from distant sources, but having a source with a known location helps the collaboration tune and improve the accuracy of its analysis methods.

\subsection{Neutrino Search with 2 Detectors} 
After 5 years of operation, the ARA collaboration carried out its first neutrino search. 
Almost 2,200 days of combined livetime were analyzed from the second and third ARA station using a cut-based analysis\index{Cut-Based Analysis} \citep{ara_a23_10mo,ara_a23}. % The actual number is 2,162 days
This type of analysis is built by defining a collection of criteria, called cuts, such that a neutrino event will pass every cut but a background event will fail at least one. 
These criteria include detector and background-related qualifications that filter out events like known calibration pulsing events, events with software glitches, and events with known or assumed anthropogenic signals. 
After these events are removed, cuts are applied to remove cosmic ray contamination. 
Cosmic rays\index{Cosmic Rays} are expected to be more commonly observed in ARA and have similar signals to neutrinos, however cosmic rays have signal that comes entirely from fairly inclined directions as these events take place above the ice and sometimes also in the first few meters of the firn. 
For example, this analysis applied a cut removing all events with a zenith less than 60$^{\circ}$ in A2 and 80$^{\circ}$ in A3. 
This is a fairly harsh cut that removes "10-30\% of neutrinos" from simulated neutrino datasets \citep{ara_a23}, so improvements to this cut involving deliberate cosmic ray identification tools are an area of active development \citep{CRiniceARENA}. 
After removing all background events that can be removed trivially, all remaining events are assumed to originate from thermal backgrounds or from real signal. 
Signal is then identified by determining if an event's reconstruction confidence and signal power is strong enough, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARA-A23-SignalCut}.

This analysis involved two pipelines with slightly different cuts and configurations. 
All events that pass these cuts are assumed to be signal-like events, though they are scrutinized further to ensure all detections are neutrino events. 
Neither pipeline found any neutrino-like events, though when all cuts are imposed on data except for the zenith cut, both found some interesting events. 
One analysis found 2 surface-like events in A2 and 1 in A3 (in addition to 3 events that are thought to be anthropogenic in origin).
The second found 7 "near surface" events in A2 and the same A3 event found in the first analysis (with 2 more events from the same run as the anthropogenic triplet found in the first analysis, so they're assumed to be background as well). 
At the time of publication, none of these surface events had been further analyzed to determine if they were cosmic ray events. 

\subsection{Phased Array Search} 
ARA's Phased Array (PA), which is connected to A5, has two main eras:
The first is when the PA and A5 were both running and the second is when the Phased Array was running on it's own with 7 of the A5 Vpols connected to the PA Data AcQuisition board (DAQ) \index {ARA Phased Array}.
The first mode ran from installation at the beginning of 2018 until the A5 DAQ suffered a fatal USB failure in October 2019. 
At the start of 2019, before the USB failure, the output from one channel connected to the A5 DAQ was split so it could be read out in both the A5 and PA DAQs which has been useful for timing calibration. 
An analysis was conducted on the 208.7 days of livetime in 2019 after the A5 channel output was split and before the A5 DAQ went down as any event triggered by the highly-sensitive PA is also readout by the A5 DAQ, who's larger baseline is advantageous for reconstructing events \citep{ara_pa_analysis}.
A cut based analysis was used for this study as well, where cuts were manually chosen by the analyzer over 10\% of the data to eliminate events of anthropogenic, cosmic ray, faulty detector, and other non-thermal and non-signal origin. 
After all cuts were chosen, tuned, and applied to 10\% of the data, all events in the 10\% sample that passed all cuts were assumed to be entirely composed of thermal background events. 
Using these thermal background events and a database of simulated neutrino events, a Fischer Discriminant was trained to identify signal and thermal noise based events to use on the full 100\% dataset \citep{fisherDiscriminant}.

\subsection{Unofficial Cosmic Ray Searches}

The ARA collaboration has identified many events in their dataset that resemble cosmic ray\index{Cosmic Ray} air showers, though none have been confirmed so far \citep{ara_a23}, \citep{ara_pa_analysis}.
These events are labeled as such due to their impulsive nature, origin above the ice, and unlikeliness of being from anthropogenic sources. 
To be observed by antennas up to 200 meters in depth, the cosmic rays must have energies greater than 10$^{16.5}$eV.
If the energy of a cosmic ray air shower is high enough and inclined enough, part of the air shower core may enter the ice and initiate an in-ice particle cascade.
This second cascade is similar in nature to the neutrino cascades ARA was designed for. 
The cascade emits radio-wavelength Askaryan Radiation\index{Askaryan Radiation} that adds coherently along the Cherenkov Cone and can be observed by the ARA stations.
