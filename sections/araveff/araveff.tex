\chapter{Calculating the Performance of the Askaryan Radio Array}

  Estimating the event rate and sensitivity of detectors such as ARA is a very important aspect to the field especially since experiments like ARA and RNO-G have yet to identify a UHE neutrino in their data. 
  Every time a detector doesn't observe an event, these simulations allow that detector to place their expectation for the upper limit of the UHE neutrino flux, allowing them to claim if the flux was greater than their sensitivity then they would've been able to observe an event. 
  These simulations typically include randomized event generation, ray tracing from the event to each antenna in a detector, and detector response to the ray-traced radiation from the event. 

  There are multiple simulation frameworks dedicated to estimating the performance of UHE neutrino detectors. 
  IceMC is the simulation framework the ARA simulation framework, AraSim is based on. 
  IceMC simulates emissions from Earth-skimming, in-ice cascades propagating to the air-borne detector ANITA. % Cite IceMC
  AraSim, built on the back of IceMC, simulates cascades in-ice and how they are observed in the ARA detector. % Cite AraSim
  Other simulation packages are more modular and allow for the simulation of multiple detectors. 
  For example, NuRadioMC is a simulation package that is primarily used by RNO-G but had been developed such that other experiments, like ARA and the European LOw-Frequency ARray, use the framework as well. % Cite NuRadioMC
  Python for Radio Experiments, PyREx, is another package that can simulate many detectors. % Cite PyREx
  Both AraSim and PyREx have been used and developed frequently over the course of my graduate school career. 
  
  An caveat of the aforementioned simulation packages is that they typically simulate events only to second order, meaning when a neutrino interacts with the ice, only the initial neutrino cascade is simulated and any additional cascades due to muons and taus resulting from charged current neutrino interactions are roughly estimated.
  AraSim and PyREx, for example, ignore muon tracks entirely and estimate tau decays by determining if the tau decay's particle cascade is greater in energy than the energy deposited in the initial cascade and, if so, directly overwriting the initial cascade with the tau decay's cascade.
  Including both the initial cascade and the tau decay's cascade and including all cascades from muon tracks not only provides extra chances for our detectors to observe Askaryan emission from a neutrino event, if one ARA station detects multiple cascades associated with the same astrophysical neutrino and if two stations detect the same cascade, it would constrain estimates on neutrino flavor, energy, and direction considerably. 
  Reconstructing neutrino flavor is an open question in this community and seeing two or more cascades directly correlated with each other strongly implies a muon or a tau charged current neutrino interaction took place. % Cite Christian Glaser's flavor paper/proceeding
  A study parameterizing neutrino secondary interactions was conducted to estimate the impact of muon tracks and tau decays. 

\section{Using NuLeptonSim to Generate Event Lists \label{sec:eventLists}}
  
  The simulation package NuLeptonSim was used to generate event lists that included initial neutrino cascades and every subsequent cascade above $10^{16}$ eV. % Todo, double check this number, cite NuLeptonSim paper
  This was done by first situating a cylinder with 15~km radius and 2.8~km depth at the center of a sheet of ice parameterized by the UNL Modified ice model that rested atop the Earth as described by the Preliminary Reference Earth Model. % Cite UNL Ice model, PREM, and give the equations for both
  This simulation volume was chosen to encompass the visibility regions all five stations in the Askaryan Radio Array so that events seen by multiple stations could be studied. 
  A uniform distribution of cords were calculated that passed through the Earth in all directions and the 100,000 that passed through the simulation volume were isolated, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:nls_trajectories}.
  10,000 lower energy neutrinos ($10^{17}$ eV and $3\times10^{17}$ eV) and 1,000 higher energy neutrinos ($10^{18}$, $3\times10^{18}$, $10^{19}$, $3\times10^{19}$, $10^{20}$, $3\times10^{20}$, $10^{21}$ eV) in each of the three neutrino flavors were simulated along these 100,000 trajectories. 
  The cross section for each neutrino was used to determine where each neutrino interacted within the earth. 
  All outgoing particles resulting from the neutrino interaction with sufficient energy were propagated along the trajectory for a distance also driven by the particle's cross section and then forced to interact. 
  Relevant parameters of all particle cascades that fell within the simulation volume were saved to a file and passed to either to PyREx or AraSim to determine which events triggered one or more ARA station, a key component in calculating effective volumes, sensitivies, and event rates.

\section{Calculating Effective Volume, Effective Area, Sensitivity, and Event Rates}

  The effective volume and effective area are a way of measuring the observational scale of a detector. 
  They are defined as the volume and surface area, respectively, of Earth that the detector could observe if it had 100\% efficiency. 
  Different collaborations and fields of study have different preferences for the usage of effective volume and effective area even though they are very similar in calculation and meaning. 
  The effective volume is defined as 
  \begin{equation}
    V_{\text{eff}} = \Omega V_i ~\frac{N_{\text{passing}}}{N_{\text{simulated}}}
    \label{eq:Veff}
  \end{equation}
  and the effective area is defined similarly as
  \begin{equation}
    A_{\text{eff}} = \pi A_i ~\frac{N_{\text{passing}}}{N_{\text{simulated}}}
    \label{eq:Aeff}
  \end{equation}
  where $V_i$ and $A_i$ are the volume and surface area where events are initialized, $N_{passing}$ is the number of events passing some criteria that is typically whether the event triggered or not in the scope of this thesis, and $N_{simulated}$ is the total number of simulated events \citep{Aeff}.
  The effective volume has an extra term of $\Omega$ that refers to the solid angle though which events enter the simulated detector from. 
  For example, many simulations simulate UHE neutrinos arriving from all directions thus arriving at the simulation volume through a solid angle of $4\pi$. 
  However, since the energies of UHE neutrinos are great enough that the Earth is opaque to them, some simulations only simulate down-going and Earth-skimming events to save on computational time. 
  Therefore, the solid angle through which events arrive in the simulation volume is slightly larger than $2\pi$ instead.
  
  There is also a conversion between effective volume and effective area sometimes used in the field of UHE neutrino astrophysics involving the interaction length, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}$, of neutrinos in ice as shown below:
  \begin{equation}
    V_{\text{eff}} = A_{\text{eff}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}
    \label{Veff_from_Aeff}
  \end{equation}
  
  The sensitivity of a detector is a characteristic designed to be used in comparison with the expected flux of particles arriving at a detector. 
  As neutrino detectors accumulate observations of neutrino cascades, they can calculate the flux of neutrinos as a function of their reconstructed energy. 
  As there have been very few UHE neutrinos observed, collaborations estimate the sensitivity of their detector and argue that the flux of UHE neutrinos must be lower than their detector's sensitivity otherwise they would have observed an event. 
  This why collaborations that have detected no neutrinos release "upper limits``- they are estimating the highest the neutrino flux could be given their understanding of their detector. 
  Collaborations with prospective detectors also plot their detector's sensitivity alongside estimate neutrino flux models to show which flux models they expect to probe or constrain.
  
  Given the steep drop in neutrino flux at ultra-high energies, neutrino flux estimates and detector sensitivities are normalized by particle energy or the square of particle energy so that features in the graph are easier to pick out. 
  For the former sensitivity calculation, the one normalized just by particle energy, the equation is 
  \todo{Figure out energy factor}
  \begin{equation}
     \Phi = 2.44 \frac{1}{ A_{\text{eff}} T } \times (\text{energy factor})
     \label{eq:sensitivity_E1}
  \end{equation}
  % Used a lot of BAC Thesis for this
  The estimated event rate for neutrinos of a given energy assuming a specific flux model, $F(E)$, is then 
  \begin{equation}
    N = \frac{F(E)}{\Phi(E)}
    \label{eq:eventRate_from_sensitivity}
  \end{equation}

\section{Simulating ARA's Performance with PyREx}

  The events generated by Ryan Krebs and Austin Cummings at Pennsylvania State University using NuLeptonSim, as described in Section~\ref{sec:eventLists}, were first ported into PyREx and results were presented at the 2023 International Cosmic Ray Conference in Nagoya, Japan.
  The setup of PyREx involved taking 1 ideal ARA station deployed to 100m (simulating ARA1), 4 ideal stations deployed to 200m (simulating ARA2, 3, 4, and 5), and 1 ideal Phased Array string deployed to 200m at the center of the ARA5 detector. 
  These detectors were deployed in the configuration they are in at the South Pole, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ARAlayout}.
  The adjective "ideal`` is used because the noise and electronic gain models are those resulting from design and testing in addition to being the same across all antennas. 
  PyREx, at the time of simulation, could not support noise and electronic gain models specific to each antenna so the ideal models were the most appropriate. 
  This involved over to 50,000 supercomputing jobs being run on clusters located all over the world through the Open Science Grid. 
  Each cascade was loaded into PyREx, ray tracing was performed between the cascade and every antenna, then the signal was propagated and convolved with the antenna and the stations' readout responses. 
  The voltage readout through each antenna was then used to determine which stations triggered. 
  Metadata was tracked for each cascade allowing us to analyze which triggered cascades are associated with other triggering cascades (e.g. multiple cascades from muon tracks). 
  Which cascades or tracks triggered multiple stations was also tracked.

  After running the simulations and processing the output, the effective area of the full Askaryan Radio Array was calculated for only initial cascades and compared to previous work. 
  The previous effective area was only for ARA station 2, so it was multiplied by 5 to achieve an order-of-magnitude estimate for the full array's effective area. % Cite the previous results
  This study found very good agreement with previous results as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:PyREx_aeff}. 
  Also shown is the breakdown of initial neutrino cascades by flavor. 
  These simulations usually simulate the neutral current interactions of all flavors since the initial cascade originates at the event interaction vertex. 
  Electron charged current interactions are simulated as well since the outgoing electron immediately incites an electromagnetic shower close enough to the event interaction vertex the electromagnetic cascade and the initial cascade are simulated together as one large shower. 
  Muon tracks and tau decays associated with muon and tau charged current interactions, respectively, involve estimates of muon stochastic losses and tau decay lengths that are typically not supported in these simulations. 
  Muon tracks are normally disregarded entirely while tau decays are only simulated if the energy of the tau decay is greater than the initial cascade, and in that scenario the initial cascade is omitted from the simulation in favor of the decay. 
  This results in a relative dominance of electron flavor effective areas in comparison to the muon and tau flavors. 
  This gives rise to the discrepancy between electron, muon, and tau flavored effective areas in Figure~\ref{fig:PyREx_aeff}. 
  It is expected that all the cascades from muon and tau stochastic losses and tau decays at higher energies will increase the muon and tau effective area relative to the electron flavored effective area as detectors will have more cascades to potentially trigger on.
